# Crew self-check guides for communications blackouts

## Important limits

These are educational, non-expert observation guides for a proposed offline reference. They are **not NASA/CSA flight procedures**, a diagnostic protocol, or a substitute for clinical evaluation. NASA and CSA public HRP/experiment pages describe risks and research; they do not publish the exact self-examination sequences below as approved blackout procedures. Before operational use, each step, device, threshold, medical supply, and escalation route must be reviewed and approved by the flight surgeon and mission operations.

Use the mission's onboard medical checklist, device instructions, and preassigned communications-blackout plan first. Stop any check that causes symptoms or is unsafe. Do not start, stop, or change medication, supplements, fluid/salt intake, exercise load, or treatment based on this guide.

### Immediate escalation symptoms

Use the approved emergency checklist and contact the onboard medical lead / ground support via approved routes for chest pain, collapse, severe breathing difficulty, new weakness or speech difficulty, seizure, confusion, sudden severe headache, acute vision loss, serious injury, severe persistent vomiting, or rapidly worsening illness. Severe flank pain, blood in urine, or urinary symptoms with fever also require prompt medical evaluation. Thoughts of self-harm or inability to remain safe require immediate crew-support escalation. Do not wait for a self-check score or a communications window.

If Earth communications are unavailable, follow the stored emergency procedure and crew roles, use only approved onboard resources, log onset and progression, attempt alternate approved communication paths, and retry on the prebriefed schedule. Escalate internally to the crew medical lead/commander and use the designated emergency resources. Never infer that “no reply” means “no emergency.”

## A. Cardiovascular and orthostatic observation

**Purpose:** Record resting pulse, blood pressure, symptoms, and change from the crewmember's approved baseline. A cuff cannot establish cardiovascular fitness or diagnose orthostatic intolerance.

1. Check that the blood-pressure cuff and pulse display are the assigned device, correctly fitted, functioning, and used according to their onboard instructions. Record device and time.
2. If safe and consistent with the approved procedure, rest quietly in the specified body orientation for the prescribed interval. In microgravity, do **not** improvise a stand-up test: “standing” is not an ordinary orthostatic challenge in orbit. Any posture change, lower-body negative-pressure device, or gravity-transition test requires an approved protocol and supervision.
3. Measure resting heart rate and blood pressure in the approved position. Keep the arm supported at the device's specified level; avoid talking or moving during measurement.
4. If the device reading is unexpected and the crew member is stable, repeat once after the prescribed rest and check cuff placement. Do not repeatedly measure if symptoms are severe.
5. Log both readings, posture, time, recent activity, relevant symptoms (lightheadedness, palpitations, breathlessness, chest discomfort), and comparison with personal baseline. Escalate symptoms or concerning trends through the approved route even if numbers appear normal.

## B. SANS visual and neurologic observation

**Purpose:** Notice new visual symptoms and record them; this is not a SANS diagnostic test. An Amsler grid is a simple central-vision check and cannot detect or rule out all eye/brain problems.

1. Use the same approved printed Amsler grid, distance, lighting, and near-vision correction each time. Do not look at the sun or use an unapproved bright light.
2. Test one eye at a time: cover (do not press) the other eye, look steadily at the center dot, and note any missing, blurred, wavy, or distorted grid areas. Repeat for the other eye.
3. Compare with the crewmember's own baseline and record which eye, location/shape of distortion, onset, and whether it is persistent. A normal grid does not clear a new symptom.
4. Record associated changes such as blurred or double vision, difficulty focusing, headache, nausea, facial fullness, or other new neurologic symptoms. Report new or worsening symptoms promptly; sudden vision loss, severe headache, confusion, weakness, or other acute neurologic signs are emergencies.
5. Do not diagnose raised intracranial pressure from “fluid shift” sensations. NASA describes headward fluid shifts as one factor associated with SANS; only authorized medical evaluation can assess the cause and significance.

## C. Bone and musculoskeletal observation

**Purpose:** Track prescribed exercise participation, device loading, discomfort, and function; this is not a bone-density measurement.

1. Before exercise, inspect the assigned Advanced Resistive Exercise Device (ARED) setup according to its equipment checklist. Do not use damaged equipment or alter a prescribed exercise plan.
2. Record the prescribed exercise, actual sets/repetitions, load setting, completion, interruptions, and any pain or equipment anomaly in the approved log. Compare with the training plan; do not increase load to compensate for missed sessions.
3. Note new localized bone pain, swelling, weakness, reduced range of motion, or a change that interferes with normal tasks. Stop an exercise that causes acute pain and use the approved reporting route.
4. Follow the mission hydration, nutrition, and urine-monitoring plan. Report reduced urine output, blood in urine, or flank pain promptly.
5. Potassium citrate is a medication, not a routine self-care supplement. Take it only if it is already prescribed and the flight medical plan explicitly directs its use. Do not self-prescribe citrate, potassium, extra fluids, or a salt change.
6. Do not infer bone density from workout loads, symptoms, or a “T-score drift.” BMD requires an approved measurement method; T-scores are not percentages.

## D. Immune and metabolic observation

**Purpose:** Create a consistent record of symptoms and authorized measurements. These checks do not determine immune status or diagnose infection, diabetes, or another metabolic disorder.

1. Wash/dry hands and use the assigned glucometer, strips, lancet, and disposal method exactly as trained. Check strip status and device prompts; do not share lancets.
2. Measure blood glucose only at the times and frequency specified by the medical plan. Record mg/dL, time, meal/activity context, symptoms, and any device error. Do not change food or medication in response unless the prescribed plan says to.
3. Measure temperature using the assigned device and specified body site. Record the value, site, time, and device; do not compare readings from different sites as if interchangeable.
4. Keep a brief symptom log: onset, duration, trend, fever/chills, sore throat, cough, rash, oral lesions, gastrointestinal symptoms, urinary symptoms, and known exposure. Record sleep, workload, and prescribed medicine as directed by the approved plan.
5. Collect or store saliva or other biological samples only when an approved study/medical procedure directs it. A symptom log is not an immune assay.
6. Report severe or worsening symptoms, fever with a deteriorating condition, confusion, breathing difficulty, inability to keep fluids down, or glucose-related severe symptoms immediately under the mission procedure.

## E. Behavioral and circadian hygiene

**Purpose:** Support routine and recognize when sleep, mood, or performance is deteriorating. This is not a psychiatric assessment or fitness-for-duty determination.

1. Follow the vehicle's mission-defined light/dark schedule and assigned work/rest periods. Use only approved lighting settings; do not independently change circadian lighting or medication schedules.
2. Keep the sleep area as dark, quiet, and comfortable as the vehicle and safety requirements allow. Reduce optional bright/blue-enriched light and stimulating activity before planned sleep when compatible with the mission schedule.
3. Record sleep opportunity and estimated actual sleep separately, along with awakenings, schedule changes, and daytime sleepiness. Wearables, if provided, are trend aids and may not measure sleep accurately in microgravity.
4. At a consistent time, record a brief mood/self-rated wellbeing score only if the mission has approved a defined scale. A score of 1–10 has no meaning unless anchors and follow-up are specified; do not treat a project-created number as a validated NASA measure.
5. Note unusual irritability, persistent low mood, anxiety, withdrawal, conflict, impaired concentration, or inability to complete routine tasks. Talk with the designated crew support person when available.
6. Escalate acute distress, thoughts of self-harm, inability to remain safe, or performance impairment that threatens immediate task safety using the mission's emergency route, even during a communications blackout.
